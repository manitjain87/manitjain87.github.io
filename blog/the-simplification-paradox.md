# The Simplification Paradox

Shopify published a product principle that reads like a paradox: "As the number and diversity of our merchants grows, what most merchants share in common becomes less and less, and thus our core product actually gets simpler, not bigger."

They called it "subtraction over addition." They added a company proverb alongside it: *"Shopify is the kind of company that will never die of starvation — only indigestion."*

This was written about a product. It describes how the best AI-era companies operate at every layer.

---

**The product layer**

The logic is counterintuitive but precise. Scale brings diversity. Diversity shrinks the shared core. What remains in the centre is only what every user actually needs — which is less than most product leaders assume. Everything else moves to the edges: apps, integrations, partner ecosystems, extensions built by others.

The pathology is responding to complexity by adding to the core. Every feature that made sense for the first 10,000 merchants becomes friction for the next 10 million. The instinct — to respond to a harder problem with a larger solution — is exactly what the proverb warns against.

The company that dies of starvation built nothing. The company that dies of indigestion built everything.

---

**The org layer**

ElevenLabs reached $450M ARR on 470 people. Mati Staniszewski — the CEO — has 15+ direct reports. So does his co-founder. Every function (ops, talent, GTM) has one technical person embedded inside it who automates workflows for that team. The complexity didn't disappear. It moved to the edges. Each function owns its own capability; the centre stays clean.

Tobi Lütke is running the same architecture at a different scale: "The small team is my bet. The 3-4-5 person team. Everyone is a 7 out of 10 on every scale now." Shopify doesn't have a central AI Centre of Excellence that every function files requests to. It has 1,000 interns teaching their managers how to work. The capability is distributed. The core is minimal.

Three sources — Shopify's documented product doctrine, ElevenLabs' operating model, Tobi's team philosophy — arrived at the same architecture independently: as scale increases, the shared coordination layer shrinks. The self-improving loop closes when you remove the middle layer that accumulates complexity instead of resolving it.

---

**The finance layer**

The most striking expression of this principle is financial. Jane Street will earn approximately $40B this year. On 4,000 people. JP Morgan earns $60B on 300,000 — nearly the same profit, 75 times the headcount. That's not a technology story. It's an architecture story. One company built a trading business with a clean, minimal operating core and extreme capability distributed to the edges. The other built a universal bank that added a function for every problem it encountered.

Both are successful. One is structurally different — and that difference compounds.

I believe that ratio is the benchmark every CFO will be asked to explain in the next five years — not by a consultant, but by a board that has seen the Jane Street number and is wondering which side of the gap their company is building toward.

---

**The AI trap**

Most companies are doing the opposite. They are adding AI tools — sometimes dozens — on top of existing workflows, existing org charts, and existing approval chains. Complexity is accumulating at the core, not the edges. The result is more surface area to manage, not more output.

The data bears this out. Cognizant found that 93% of jobs have already been disrupted by AI — six years ahead of its own forecast. The promised productivity gains never arrived. Researchers called it the "activation gap." Companies have the tools. They don't have the clean operating core for those tools to run on.

Forty tools. Twelve agents. Three vendors. A Centre of Excellence that fields requests from functions whose data pipelines are still too tangled for AI to do anything useful with. That is not an AI adoption problem. It is a simplification problem that AI just made visible.

---

**Ground Reality:**

The Shopify principle does not say *remove capability*. It says move complexity to the edges and keep the centre clean. For an org, that means fewer central mandates and more embedded capability per function. For a finance team, it means fewer dashboards and more models that make decisions automatic. For a CFO, it means asking one honest question: is my team the clean core — or the accumulated complexity?

The companies that understand this will get closer to the Jane Street end of the ratio. The ones that respond to the AI era by adding more AI on top of existing complexity will hit the indigestion wall before they hit the transformation.

**The honest take:** The most valuable thing most AI-era CFOs could do right now is not implement a new AI tool. It is subtract one reporting layer, one approval chain, one reconciliation step that no one would notice missing. Start there. Then let AI run on what's left.
