Four of the most cash-generative companies in history are now funding their AI build from the capital markets — and not all of it in a form a screen will show you.

Infrastructure spending absorbed 96 cents of every dollar Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta generated from operations last quarter. $171.8bn came in. $164.9bn went out. $6.9bn was left between the four of them, and essentially all of it belongs to one.

It doesn't stop there. In the first half of 2026 Alphabet raised a net $86.3bn and Amazon $62.9bn. Alphabet stopped buying back its own shares entirely and sold new ones instead — $30.5bn of stock, plus $19.1bn of preferred paying 6.25%.

Microsoft did something easier to miss. Its debt actually fell. It signed leases instead: $329.1bn of data centre leases that have not yet started, sitting on no balance sheet at all.

So what is the return? On Microsoft's July earnings call, a Goldman Sachs analyst asked CFO Amy Hood how the ROI on today's AI spending compares with a year ago.

Her answer: "I don't know that, quite frankly, my math has changed in terms of how I do it over the past year."

The revenue on the other side is moving too. Anthropic took between 62% and 84% of every dollar spent through Vercel's AI Gateway — the one API apps call instead of wiring up every model provider — for eleven straight months. In September it took 45.8%; across the first nine days of October, 40.6%. I pulled the raw export on 10 October, 22,869 rows, and ran the series myself.

The streak broke. In August, open-weight models ran most of the tokens on that gateway, on about 14 cents of every dollar.

Microsoft and Meta are moving their own engineers off Claude too — per The Information, Meta's Claude Code users went from about 60,000 to 30,000 and Microsoft cut a billion-dollar internal budget by over a third. Neither has confirmed it.

The bill, though, is contracted. Per Reuters, which says it has seen Anthropic's confidential prospectus: at least $518bn committed over a decade, about 80% non-cancelable — $111.1bn to Google, $110bn to Amazon, $31.4bn to Microsoft, payable whether it uses the compute or not.

So "long AI" is not one position. It is at least four, and they do not fail together.

Part Two — The Counterparty. Whether that revenue is collectible.

Part Three — What Survives the Counterparty. Why the infrastructure gets used even if the company that underwrote it doesn't.

Part Four — The Credit Layer. How the four biggest buyers paid for this, and who holds the risk. Search the SEC's database for Anthropic and you get 35 filers. Every one is a fund that exists to buy its shares. Not one of them is Anthropic.

## PART ONE — Ninety-Six Cents

On Microsoft's 29 July earnings call, Goldman's Gabriela Borges asked Amy Hood how the ROI on today's capex decisions compares with a year ago.

Her answer: "I don't know that, quite frankly, my math has changed in terms of how I do it over the past year."

No number. No payback period. No framework.

Here is the number she didn't give. Across Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta, capital expenditure absorbed 96 cents of every dollar those four generated from operations in the June quarter.

$171.8bn of operating cash flow in. $164.9bn of capex out. $6.9bn of free cash flow left — and the positive aggregate is essentially one company. Alphabet spent 115% of its operating cash flow. Amazon 117%.

Capex guidance is a plan, announced with a press release. The cash flow statement is the outcome. The debate is being argued on the wrong line of the P&L.

Position in the chain now matters more than a view on AI. Micron spent 25% of its operating cash flow on capex. NVIDIA 11%. The four buyers 96%. CoreWeave's interest bill ran at five times its operating profit.

Identical demand. Four completely different cash profiles. Four different tolerances for being early.

And the ranking is inverted. SemiAnalysis rates CoreWeave and Nebius the best AI clouds in the industry — ahead of every hyperscaler — and they are the two that have to borrow to stay there.

Operating quality and balance-sheet quality are different variables. This cycle prices them as one.

"Long AI infrastructure" is not one position. It is at least four, and they do not fail together.

## PART TWO — The Counterparty

Anthropic took between 62% and 84% of every dollar spent through Vercel's AI Gateway. Eleven months straight.

In September it took 45.8%. Across the first nine days of October, 40.6%.

Vercel hosts web apps and builds Next.js. Its AI Gateway is the one API those apps call instead of wiring up every model provider, and it publishes the spend data openly. Vercel's own report notes the share never dropped below 61% since December 2025. So I pulled the raw export on 10 October — 22,869 rows back to October 2025 — and ran the monthly series myself.

The streak broke.

By August, open-weight models ran most tokens on that gateway — on 14 cents of every dollar. In December, 7%.

What surprised me: roughly half of what Anthropic lost in September went to OpenAI, not to open source. GPT-6 Astra launched on 3 September and took a third of OpenAI's gateway spend within 48 hours.

Microsoft and Meta are also moving their own engineers off Claude — per The Information, Meta's Claude Code users went from about 60,000 to 30,000 and Microsoft cut a billion-dollar internal budget by over a third. Neither has confirmed it.

Google proves it can happen to anyone. Its share of gateway tokens fell from 39% in April to 5% in September.

Now the bill. Per Reuters, which says it has seen Anthropic’s confidential prospectus: at least $518 billion committed over a decade, about 80% of it non-cancelable. $111.1bn to Google, $110bn to Amazon, $31.4bn to Microsoft — payable whether it uses the compute or not.

The growth is genuinely historic. Revenue of $4.73bn in Q1 2026, $11.6bn in Q2 — up 145% — and $16.8bn in Q3. That Q3 figure, plus a first adjusted-profitable quarter, came out on 14 October at a closed-door pre-IPO investor day via the Financial Times. Nobody outside the room has been told whether it is gross or net, or what "adjusted" means.

The rate is also decelerating, on both measures. Quarterly revenue: +145%, then +45%. Monthly run-rate: 36% to 58% through the spring, then 17% to 24% since May. Still huge. Roughly a third of the quarterly rate.

And run rate is not revenue. A $100bn run rate is one month annualised. Adding the three reported quarters gives about $33.1bn for nine months — my arithmetic on three reported, unaudited, unfiled figures. That $518bn bill averages $51.8bn a year.

Nine months of revenue against two thirds of a single year's average bill. It works while the compounding holds. The commitments do not decelerate. The growth rate already has.

Two caveats. Anthropic books cloud-reseller revenue gross, which flatters the top line against how OpenAI reports. And its first profitable quarter landed inside a compute discount window — though it says the next was profitable too, at the full rate.

The post also works through what happens if it lists at $2 trillion, and why OpenAI is better supplied and more heavily committed.

## PART THREE — What Survives the Counterparty

On 2 September NVIDIA signed a definitive agreement to acquire Hugging Face — a headline $12.93 billion — and in the same filing wrote, in a risk factor of its own:

"Many of the world's most popular and successful open-source models originated in China and are then downloaded, revised, fine-tuned, and tested by developers in the United States and worldwide."

That is the place open models are distributed from, and the buyer is the company with the most to lose from being wrong about them. As of 12 October it has not closed. It is also the best evidence for an argument I did not expect to end up making.

The infrastructure gets used even if the company that underwrote it does not.

Those are not contradictory, because the thing that uses the compute does not have to be the thing that paid for it. Gavin Baker has made the case directly: margin compression at the frontier labs is net positive for power, semiconductors, hyperscalers and neoclouds, because cheaper models raise intelligence per dollar and therefore volume.

In August, open-weight models ran 56% of all tokens on Vercel's AI Gateway on about 14 cents of every dollar. Read one way that is a failure to monetise. Read the other way it is the floor: if the frontier labs re-price, the workloads do not vanish — they move down the cost curve onto exactly the capacity that was built for them.

The weakness is timing, not direction.

And the timing is where the disagreement actually sits. Amazon says it will still not have enough capacity to meet demand in 2026, expects that to "also be true in 2027," and has the "lion's share" of 2027 capacity "largely reserved" with "quite a bit" already reserved for 2028.

Meta explicitly declines to guide 2027 and has built a deliberately optional 2028 — land and power now, chips decided later.

Micron dates the supply response and it lands after the cliff: the cleanrooms its fiscal 2027 construction funds come online late 2028 and beyond, with industry DRAM bit growth decelerating to low-20s% in 2027 and 2028.

So the argument in this sector is not about demand. Everybody agrees demand exceeds supply; nobody is differentiated by that view. The differentiated question is 2028, and the best-informed participants do not agree.

One honest caveat on my own thesis. Reflection AI has committed up to $6.3bn to SpaceX and over $1bn to Nebius against roughly $4.6bn raised, and its open-weight release is promised rather than shipped. Commitments exceeding capital is the exact pattern that makes Anthropic fragile.
