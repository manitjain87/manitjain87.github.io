The Front Door Problem

September 28, 2026 · 19 min read

Disclosure: I hold shares of Uber (UBER). This is not investment advice.

In November 2025, on The Best One Yet podcast, Lyft CEO David Risher described his company's partnership with Waymo as a "situationship."

It was a joke. It was also the most accurate description of robotaxi economics anyone has offered. Whoever controls the car, the software and — increasingly — the rider relationship holds the leverage. Everyone else is dating on its terms.

On July 24, 2026, Uber found out what that means. Waymo told Uber it will launch its own app in Austin and Atlanta in January 2028 — the only two US cities where Waymo rides are sold exclusively through Uber. Uber's stock fell more than 4% that day, closing below $66, its lowest level in over a year.

This post is about what that split actually tells us. Not about cars — about a question every company building on, or reselling, someone else's AI is going to face:

When your best supplier can build its own front door, what is the storefront worth?

My answer: less than the bulls think in the handful of cities where the best supplier is strong — and more than the market is pricing almost everywhere else.

What actually happened (and what didn't)

The headlines made this sound like a divorce. The documents read more like a slow renegotiation in public.

Phoenix, May 2026. Uber and Waymo quietly ended their Phoenix pilot. It sounded bigger than it was: Uber says the program peaked at "just over a dozen vehicles." Waymo folded them back into its own fleet — which also delivers for DoorDash, Uber Eats' largest US competitor. Uber says it will announce a new, unnamed self-driving partner for Phoenix.

Austin and Atlanta, July 2026. Waymo gave notice it will launch its own app in both cities in January 2028, alongside Uber. The existing contract keeps "hundreds" of Waymo robotaxis on Uber through at least May 2028. Uber's spokesperson pointed to the other side of the coin: the end of exclusivity frees Uber to bring other self-driving providers into both cities.

Why. Per the Financial Times, both sides had grievances. Waymo complained about vehicle cleanliness and routing — including a May incident in which dozens of empty Waymos clogged an Atlanta cul-de-sac, which Waymo blamed on Uber's dispatch. Uber reportedly argued the deal had "unsustainable financial terms," objected to Waymos going offline in bad weather, and — per Bloomberg — learned about Waymo's school-bus problems in Austin from the news rather than from Waymo.

Those school-bus incidents are often conflated, so it's worth being precise. Austin's school district documented at least two dozen cases of Waymos illegally passing stopped school buses during the 2025–26 school year; none of the documented Austin cases involved a collision. Waymo issued a voluntary software recall covering 3,067 vehicles in December 2025, and both NHTSA and the NTSB opened investigations. Separately, in January 2026, a Waymo struck a child who darted into the road outside a school in Santa Monica, California; the child had minor injuries.

Two different incidents, two different states — and neither caused July's stock drop.

What moved the stock was not safety. It was the realisation that the best supplier in the category had decided it no longer needed the distribution deal.

One more detail that tells you this is a renegotiation, not a rupture: on September 24, two months after the notice, Waymo expanded fully driverless freeway rides in Austin — bookable through the Uber app.

How big is the hole?

Here is the part the bear case gets right.

Waymo is no longer a science project. It now sells rides in 15 US cities — four of them (Denver, San Diego, Tampa, Las Vegas) switched on this September alone — and averages about 500,000 paid rides a week, with a stated goal of 1 million by year-end. Of those 15 cities, only Austin and Atlanta run through Uber. In February it raised $16 billion at a $126 billion valuation.

And in the last few months it launched Waymo Premier — a $29.99-a-month subscription for priority pickups and ride credits. If that sounds familiar, it should. It's Waymo building its own version of Uber One.

That is not what a supplier does when it plans to stay a supplier.

Riders are paying up for it. Obi, which aggregates real-time ride prices, tracked the Waymo premium in San Francisco. In April 2025, a Waymo cost about 31% more than a comparable UberX. By late 2025 the gap had narrowed to about 13% ($19.69 vs. $17.47) — which looked like the novelty wearing off. Then it reversed: between December and April 2026, Waymo's average fare rose 18% while Uber's rose 5%, pushing the premium back up to roughly a quarter.

A product that can raise prices faster than the incumbent while growing isn't losing its pull. (Tesla's robotaxi, still running with a safety driver in San Francisco, sits at the other extreme — roughly half Waymo's price.)

In San Francisco, the same data shows Waymo at about 20% a year earlier, in June 2025. Roughly one in every six or seven ride-hail dollars inside Waymo's zones now goes to a car with nobody in the driver's seat.

Uber reads the same markets very differently. From its August 5 earnings remarks: "in more mature AV markets including Los Angeles, San Francisco, and Phoenix, Uber's category share, both citywide and in AV operating zones, is higher today than it was a year ago."

Both can be true — and that's the honest problem with the data. Yipit only counts trips that start and end inside Waymo's zones. Every time Waymo expands (its Bay Area zone now runs 260+ square miles, San Francisco to San Jose), the denominator absorbs new territory where Uber is already entrenched and Waymo is starting from zero. Waymo's share falls partly by construction — which is exactly what the table shows.

Meanwhile Lyft says its rides inside San Francisco's AV zone grew about 20% year over year — a hint that robotaxis may be growing the category, not just splitting it.

Nobody outside these three companies knows the real number. Anyone who tells you they do is selling something.

Uber's counter-argument is an AI argument

This is where it gets interesting for anyone who doesn't care about cars.

In its August 5 prepared remarks, Uber made its case for surviving Waymo going direct — and it didn't reach for a transportation analogy. It reached for AI:

"A few years ago, many observers expected AI to converge around a single foundation model. Instead, we've seen multiple frontier models emerge… Increasingly, applications use the right model for the right task, and model providers distribute both directly and through third-party platforms. We believe autonomous mobility is likely to evolve in a similar way."

Read that again with your own vendor stack in mind. OpenAI sells ChatGPT to consumers directly, while its models are also sold to enterprises through Microsoft Azure. Anthropic sells Claude directly, and through Amazon Bedrock and Google Cloud's Vertex AI. Nobody thinks the cloud platforms are finished because the model labs have their own apps.

The question is only who captures the margin on which customers. Uber's bet is that robotaxis end up with the same structure: first-party apps and third-party platforms, side by side.

The playbook: three bets, one number

On September 10, at Goldman Sachs' Communacopia conference, CEO Dara Khosrowshahi laid out how Uber intends to win that structure. Three prongs, in his words:

1. "Partner with every single AV provider, technology provider out there." Waymo, Zoox, WeRide, Pony.ai, Avride "and many other players." Uber doesn't bet on which self-driving technology wins; it tries to aggregate demand for whichever ones do. It now counts more than 30 autonomous-vehicle partners across rides, delivery and freight, and has taken equity stakes in several (it owns more than 11% of Lucid).

2. Secure the cars — and finance them like data centers. "We have secured access to over 120,000 vehicles… What we are seeing is the opportunity to finance those vehicles through finance partners. Just like you see all the data center financing showing up." Uber has said it expects to commit more than $10 billion over the coming years across equity stakes, infrastructure and vehicle purchase commitments — while bringing in outside capital to carry the vehicles.

3. Own the unglamorous layer: depots, charging, maintenance. Uber committed more than $100 million in February to fast-charging hubs at its own robotaxi depots, starting in the Bay Area, Los Angeles and Dallas. It has signed a 50,000-square-foot depot in Houston for a 2027 launch. And it multi-sources the day-to-day work: Avomo (part of Moove, in which Uber holds a stake) runs the Waymo fleet operations in Austin and Atlanta; Hertz's new Oro Mobility unit will charge, clean and repair the Lucid–Nuro robotaxis launching in the Bay Area.

The timeline, also on the record: "We are operating now in markets with nine partners. We think we will get to 15 by the end of the year." 2027 is about proving fully driverless partnerships — cars with no safety operator — in the US. "2028, 2029 is all about scaling." Goldman's host summarised Uber's stated ambition: to be "the largest facilitator" of self-driving rides by 2029.

All three bets rest on one number.

The shift from variable cost to fixed cost. Today, Uber pays drivers only when they drive. Idle time costs Uber nothing — the driver absorbs it. A robotaxi flips that. Khosrowshahi says partners now have "line of sight to a $50,000, $60,000 cost" per vehicle, including sensors and compute. That car costs money every hour it sits empty — plus the depot, charging and cleaning behind it.

When the cost base goes from variable to fixed, the single number that decides profitability is utilization: the share of the day a car spends carrying a paying rider.

Khosrowshahi's claim: because Uber already has so much demand flowing through its app, an idle car is almost always near a rider. "Where there is a human really close, we will dispatch a human, but where there is demand for an AV really close, we will dispatch an AV." The result, he says, is "significantly higher utilization [than] is possible for, let us say, a 1P player" — a single company running only its own fleet through only its own app. "We estimate by 30%."

Then an additional edge, on top of the 30%: several brands' cars can share one Uber depot, spreading the fixed cost of repair and charging further than a one-brand operator can.

Then the part I find most convincing, because it's a marketplace argument rather than a technology one: the hybrid fleet. Size the robotaxi fleet for steady, everyday demand so every car stays busy — and let human drivers flex in for the spikes. Rush hour. A storm. "A Taylor Swift concert." A single-brand operator has to either buy enough cars for the peak and eat the idle cost the rest of the week, or leave riders stranded when demand spikes.

Anyone who has run a marketplace P&L will recognise this as the classic 1P-versus-3P stock decision. First-party (1P) inventory is stock you own — you carry its cost every day, whether it sells or not. Third-party (3P) inventory sits on your platform but on someone else's balance sheet — you earn when it sells and carry nothing when it doesn't. A robotaxi fleet is 1P stock with wheels. Uber's hybrid model is a marketplace deciding how much 1P to hold for the base load, and letting 3P supply cover the peaks.

The picks-and-shovels bet most people miss

There's a second way Uber can win that doesn't depend on riders opening its app at all.

In February 2026, Uber launched Uber Autonomous Solutions — a separate unit, led by Sarfraz Maredia, that sells the hard, boring parts of running a robotaxi business to any self-driving company that wants them: training data, mapping, help with regulators, vehicle financing, fleet management software, insurance, remote assistance and customer support.

The data piece is concrete. Uber's AV Labs team is deploying hundreds of sensor-equipped cars that collect millions of miles of driving data a month for partners to train on. Wayve, the UK self-driving startup, is on the record saying Uber's multi-city data adds diversity its models need.

This is a deliberate choice about what Uber won't do. It tried building its own self-driving car and sold that unit in 2020. Now, as The Verge put it, it does "basically everything it can do except design and build the vehicles itself."

The same logic extends beyond passenger cars — to self-driving trucks through Uber Freight (Waabi), sidewalk delivery robots (Starship) and drones (Flytrex).

If you are selling shovels, you don't need to know which miner strikes gold. You need miners.

Outside the US, the map looks different

In the US, Waymo has something no one else has: a consumer brand people trust enough to ride without a driver. In most of the world, no operator has that yet — which is exactly where an aggregator matters most.

  • Middle East: WeRide and Uber plan at least 1,200 robotaxis across Abu Dhabi, Dubai and Riyadh, as soon as 2027.
  • Europe: WeRide received Spain's first national permit for driverless testing this month — Madrid will be the fourth of 15 WeRide–Uber cities, with commercial service targeted by end-2026. Pony.ai and Croatia's Verne are launching in Zagreb. Momenta is preparing Munich, pending approval.
  • Asia: Uber, Nissan and Wayve plan a Tokyo pilot for late 2026.

These are announcements and early deployments — not yet material revenue. But they share a pattern: a technology company without a local consumer brand, plugging into a platform that already has riders.

Now watch what Waymo does abroad. In London, it's launching through its own app — while Uber launches with Wayve. That's the first city where the two models go head-to-head from day one.

In Tokyo, Waymo announced this month it will launch in 2027 with GO, Japan's leading taxi app, and taxi operator Nihon Kotsu — riders will be able to book through either GO or Waymo's app.

Read that twice. Where Waymo has no brand and no local operations, the world's most advanced self-driving company chose… a local aggregator. It just didn't choose Uber.

Where Uber's argument is weakest

A 30% utilization edge is a management estimate, not an audited number. Four things cut against the bull case.

The direction of travel is not in doubt. The most advanced operator in the category is moving away from Uber in the US, city by city, on a dated schedule. Phoenix is gone; Austin and Atlanta have an end date. The bull case doesn't just need Waymo to stop drifting — it needs Uber's other partners to more than replace it.

The replacements aren't on the road yet. As of August, self-driving rides were live on Uber in 7 cities, with a target of up to 15 by year-end. Several headline partners — Nuro/Lucid, Rivian, Volkswagen's MOIA — have no scaled commercial service. Rivian's robotaxis aren't due in San Francisco and Miami until 2028. A committed vehicle is not a car on the road.

The timing gap. Bank of America's Justin Post expects Uber and Lyft's robotaxi operations to start scaling in 2028 — which, by his estimate, gives rivals an 18-to-24-month window to build fleets and habits first.

The habit risk. The real danger isn't losing Waymo's cars. It's losing the reflex of opening Uber first. Uber One and Waymo Premier are now competing for the same thing: being the default app on a rider's home screen.

How much of this is already in the price?

The known losses are small and dated: Phoenix (a dozen cars, already gone) and the exclusivity in Austin and Atlanta (from January 2028, with Uber access through at least May 2028). The market has already reacted — Uber closed 2025 at $81.71 and sits around $70 today, down roughly 15% this year, with July 24 the sharpest single blow.

So the useful question isn't "will Waymo leave Uber?" It already has, in three cities. The useful question is "how far does it spread?" That's how I'd frame the scenarios:

  • Bear: de-platforming spreads beyond these three cities — another major US metro, or a shift in San Francisco, Los Angeles or Miami — and robotaxi growth stays with direct operators.
  • Base: the three known cities are the extent of it; Uber replaces lost Waymo economics with other partners, at similar economics, over the next five years. That's Uber's stated strategy — not yet proven at scale.
  • Bull: the pattern stops at three cities and the utilization edge proves real — Uber becomes the default commercial layer for everyone except the one or two players big enough to go it alone.

The single most useful thing to watch is therefore not share data (contested) or price data (noisy). It's whether a fourth major US metro gets a dated own-app announcement that pulls it off Uber.

Management has made its view clear. On September 10, Khosrowshahi said flatly: "we think our stock is really cheap." The same day, he bought 141,000 shares on the open market at about $70.96 — roughly $10 million, not under a pre-arranged trading plan. For balance: he sold about $43.7 million of stock over the prior twelve months. One purchase is a signal, not a verdict.

A private company whose finances aren't public is valued at nearly 90% of Uber's entire market capitalisation. Tesla — which generated roughly 43% less free cash flow than Uber over the past year, and burned $1.1 billion in its latest quarter — is valued at more than ten times Uber.

The fair steelman: Waymo and Tesla aren't priced on today's cash. They're priced as long-dated options on replacing a very large share of all driving. You can't compare an option to a cash machine on a trailing multiple and declare the cash machine cheap.

But that cuts both ways. The market seems to be pricing "robotaxis disrupt ride-hailing" as a single, binary outcome. The evidence shows something messier and more local: a city-by-city split, with Waymo going direct where it has scale and brand, and aggregators — Uber, or GO in Tokyo — carrying the rest.

The parts of Uber nobody models as a robotaxi story

Most of the robotaxi debate treats Uber as a ride-hailing company with a Waymo problem. The business underneath is broader — and some of it is directly relevant to the habit fight.

Membership. Uber One has more than 50 million members, growing about 50% a year, and accounts for roughly half of Uber's bookings — closer to 60% in some markets. Members spend three times more than non-members. Khosrowshahi describes it as both offense and defense: in price wars, "member category position stays very consistent," while less loyal non-members drift. That is the moat Waymo Premier has to breach.

Platform acquisition. Customers who use more than one Uber product spend three times more than single-product customers. That lets Uber pay more to acquire a customer than a rides-only or delivery-only rival — at the same return. His words: "relatively new in our skill set."

The barbell. High-margin products (Uber for Business, Uber Black, priority delivery, advertising) fund low-cost products (two- and three-wheelers, Wait & Save, grocery promotions) that bring in new customers — who are then upsold.

Advertising. More than a $2.5 billion annual run-rate, still growing about 50% a year, now spreading from restaurant listings into grocery and in-ride formats. Advertising carries no driver or vehicle cost, so it's structurally higher-margin than the core marketplace — though Uber doesn't disclose its margin.

Grocery and retail. A $15 billion category growing over 40%. Khosrowshahi: it "can be bigger than our core online food delivery business."

And a leaner cost base. In early September Uber cut about 10% of its full-time staff — roughly 3,300 roles — framed as removing management layers "from a position of strength," with savings going back into lower prices and selection.

None of this is a robotaxi. All of it bears on who owns the rider's habit when robotaxis arrive.

Why this matters if you've never thought about robotaxis

Take the cars out of this story and you get a pattern that's about to show up in every AI budget.

If you are the aggregator — the platform, the reseller, the company that wraps someone else's model in a product — your moat is not access to the best supplier. Access lasts until the supplier decides it doesn't need you. Your moat is the economics you deliver that the supplier can't deliver alone: demand density, utilization, the ability to route each job to whichever provider is cheapest or best for it. Uber's pitch to robotaxi makers is exactly the pitch a good AI platform makes to model labs.

If you are the buyer — a CFO signing an enterprise AI contract — the Waymo split is a reminder that your "partner" can also become a competitor for the same customer. Ask the question Uber should have priced in 2023: what happens to our economics when the supplier launches its own front door?

If you are the supplier — you go direct where you are scarce and trusted, and you distribute through someone else's platform where you are not. Waymo goes direct in Phoenix and San Francisco. In Tokyo it plugs into GO.

What to watch — with dates

  • Early November 2026 — Uber's Q3 filing. The first place any balance-sheet commitment tied to financing the 120,000 vehicles would have to show up.
  • Year-end 2026 — whether Uber hits 15 self-driving partners in market (nine as of September), and Lucid–Nuro rides go live in the Bay Area.
  • London, now — Waymo's own app versus Uber + Wayve, in the same city. The cleanest head-to-head test of first-party versus aggregator anywhere.
  • Any day — a fourth major US metro getting a dated Waymo (or other operator) own-app launch that pulls it off Uber. The single best signal the split is spreading.
  • Regulation — Uber is lobbying for "hybrid network" rules, including a New Jersey proposal requiring human drivers to provide at least 85% of rides during a three-year pilot. Rules like that would push direct-only operators onto shared platforms. Watch which way states go.
  • 2027 — Uber's first fully driverless partnerships in the US, a Houston launch, and Waymo in Tokyo via GO.
  • January 2028 — Waymo's own app goes live in Austin and Atlanta.
  • May 2028 — the Uber–Waymo contract in those cities runs out. The first real test of how many riders follow the car, and how many stay with the app.

The Honest Take

The direction of travel with Waymo is clear, and it would be dishonest to soften it: the most advanced self-driving company in the world is moving away from Uber in the US, city by city, on a dated schedule, while building its own membership program. None of that is noise.

But "Waymo doesn't need Uber in San Francisco" is a much smaller claim than "robotaxis make Uber obsolete." The first is true today. The second assumes one company wins everywhere, at once, on its own balance sheet — carrying the cost of every idle car, every depot and every rush-hour peak by itself. Waymo's own Tokyo deal suggests even Waymo doesn't believe that.

I believe that when a cost base goes from variable to fixed, the operator who keeps the asset busiest wins the economics — and that is an aggregator's game, not a single brand's. Uber still has to prove it by putting cars on the road, not just announcing partners. The dates above will tell us whether it can.

The same test is coming for every company in the AI supply chain. When your best supplier builds its own front door, the only question that matters is whether your door delivers economics theirs can't.
