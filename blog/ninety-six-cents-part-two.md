# The Counterparty

Vercel publishes, openly and daily under a Creative Commons licence, how spending on its AI Gateway splits between model providers. On Vercel’s own measure, Anthropic’s share of those dollars never fell below 61% in any month since December 2025.

In September it was **45.8%**. I pulled the raw export on 10 October and computed the monthly series myself; across the first nine days of October it ran at **40.6%**.

[Part One](ninety-six-cents.html) established that the cash went out: four hyperscalers converted **$171.8 billion** of operating cash flow into **$6.9 billion** of free cash flow. The case for spending it rests on contracted revenue — and a material, named slice of three cloud backlogs is one counterparty, contractually bound. This part is about whether that revenue is collectible.

*Second of four. **[Part One](ninety-six-cents.html)** · **[Part Three](ninety-six-cents-part-three.html)** · **[Part Four](ninety-six-cents-part-four.html)**. Each part stands on its own.*

*Part Two sits at a lower and more varied bar, deliberately marked where it appears. Anthropic's prospectus figures are Reuters' reporting of a confidential draft nobody outside Reuters has read. The Vercel AI Gateway figures are published production data under an open licence, which I pulled and computed myself. The Microsoft and Meta usage cuts are a single press report. I'd rather separate those than blend them.*

**PART TWO — Whether whoever took it keeps it**

---

## The backlog, and whether it is collectible

The demand claim is not only adjectives: all three cloud businesses disclosed a contracted figure this season. They are large, they are not the same metric, and a material named slice of all three is one customer.

| Company | Contracted metric | Value | Growth | Conversion disclosed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Microsoft | Commercial RPO | **$678B** | +84% YoY; **+25% excluding OpenAI** | ~30% in next 12 months; weighted-average duration 2.3 yrs |
| Alphabet | Google Cloud backlog | **$514B** | +$50B sequentially | *"just over 50%"* over next 24 months |
| Amazon | AWS backlog | **$496B** | *"growing triple digits"* YoY | not quantified |

Microsoft's RPO growth flatters on the headline — **+84% including OpenAI, +25% without it.** Both are Microsoft's own figures; the gap is the concentration.

And these three are not the same metric. RPO is an accounting-defined disclosure; "backlog" at Alphabet and AWS is company-defined, and only two of the three published a recognition schedule. You will see them added into a single trillion-dollar "committed AI revenue" number. That sum combines three definitions and one unknown conversion rate.

**And we now know who a large slice of that backlog is.** On 29 September, Reuters reported having seen Anthropic's confidential IPO prospectus. It discloses plans to spend **at least $518 billion over a decade** on AI infrastructure with six partners — of which **roughly 80% is non-cancelable or payable regardless of usage.** Six partners, of which the four largest are **$111.1 billion with Google, $110 billion with Amazon, $31.4 billion with Microsoft** — explicitly *"regardless of usage"* — and about **$161.2 billion** of Broadcom-related equipment leases. Those four are the non-cancelable ones and they sum to **$413.7 billion, or 79.8% of the total**, which is where the "about 80%" comes from. The remaining two are softer: about **$84.5 billion with xAI**, largely cancelable on 90 days' notice, and **$20 billion with AMD**.

Read that against the table above. A material, named portion of Microsoft's RPO, Alphabet's backlog and AWS's backlog is one counterparty, contractually bound.

Then read the other side of Anthropic's own disclosure. 2025 revenue of **$4.59 billion** against an operating loss that widened to **$8.06 billion**; compute and infrastructure spend of **$7.33 billion** — roughly 1.6× revenue and 58% of total operating expenses; **$20.28 billion** of cash and short-term investments. Anthropic reportedly warned that nearly a quarter of revenue came from two customers, and that many of its largest clients are **not** bound by long-term contracts and could cut spending.

So the cost side is locked for a decade and the revenue side is not. That asymmetry is the single most important thing disclosed this autumn, and it is the thing the hyperscaler backlog tables cannot show you.

*Sourcing caveat, stated plainly: the prospectus is a confidential draft. It is not on EDGAR and the public cannot read it. Every Anthropic figure here traces to Reuters' reporting of a document I have not seen — a different standard from everything else in this post, and I'm flagging it rather than blending it in.*

**And two of those customers started walking in October.** The Information reported on 5 October that Meta and Microsoft are both steering staff off Claude. Meta's Claude Code users fell from roughly **60,000 to 30,000**; Microsoft cut projected internal Anthropic spend of **at least $1 billion by more than a third**, with the cloud division's per-employee monthly budget dropping from **$100,000 to about $10,000**. Neither company has confirmed it, and it applies to their own engineers, not their customers — Microsoft's enterprise customers reportedly kept increasing Claude spend.

Both are still large Anthropic customers. Meta spent over **$105 million on Claude Code in a recent 28-day period**. But the reason they are cutting is the part that matters: they are substituting their own tools. Microsoft is pushing engineers to GitHub Copilot CLI and its MAI models. Meta now has **MetaCode with more than 30,000 internal users** and is testing Muse Code externally.

So Microsoft is simultaneously owed **$31.4 billion** by Anthropic regardless of usage, and weaning its own engineers off the product. That is not a customer relationship. It is two companies on opposite sides of the same contract.

**Now the growth rate, because it is the whole argument.** Anthropic's revenue went from **$4.73 billion in Q1 2026 to $11.6 billion in Q2** — up **145%** in a quarter — and then to **$16.8 billion in Q3**, up **45%**. The Q3 figure, and a first quarter of profitability on an adjusted basis, came out on **14 October** at a closed-door pre-IPO investor day, reported by the *Financial Times* through an anonymous source. Nobody outside that room has been told whether the $16.8 billion is gross or net, or what "adjusted" means, and both of those gaps matter more than the number. Its run-rate went from about **$9 billion at the end of 2025** (the company's own figure) to **$47 billion by May**, about $65 billion in late July, and **past $100 billion by September** — that last mark reported by the *New York Times* citing unnamed sources, not stated by Anthropic.

Those are extraordinary numbers. They are also decelerating, and now on two independent measures. On quarterly revenue: **+145%, then +45%.** On monthly run-rate: **36% to 58% a month through February to May**, then roughly **17% to 24% a month from May to September**. Still enormous. Roughly a third of the quarterly rate, and about half the monthly one.

Here is the trap in reading any of it. **Run-rate is not revenue.** A $100 billion run-rate is one month annualised. Add the three reported quarters and nine-month 2026 revenue is about **$33.1 billion** — my arithmetic on three reported figures, none of them audited and none filed. Put that against the bill: **$518 billion over a decade averages $51.8 billion a year.** Nine months of revenue at the company's best-ever run rate is roughly two thirds of one year of the average commitment. If Q4 grows between 25% and 45%, full-year 2026 lands near **$54–57 billion**, inside the **$50–65 billion** range the earlier two quarters implied.

Set that against $518 billion over a decade, about 80% non-cancelable. That is roughly **$52 billion a year, on average, that must be paid.**

Which means 2026 revenue is roughly equal to the annual average of the bill. It works only while the compounding continues. The commitments do not decelerate. The growth rate already has.

### What the equity is worth, and what happens if it lists

Anthropic's last mark is **$965 billion** post-money — Series H, $65 billion raised, 28 May 2026, on the company's own page. The $2 trillion figure in circulation is not a mark. It is an **IPO target** attributed to bankers.

Resist the easy version of the argument. Bankers are comping Palantir at **53x** and SpaceX and Cloudflare at **41.6x** EV/2026 revenue. Against the **$50–65 billion** that Anthropic's three reported quarters imply for 2026, $2 trillion is roughly **30–40x** — inside that range, not above it. Anyone calling the multiple absurd has not done the division.

The problem is not the multiple. It is what the multiple is struck against. **$2 trillion rests on Anthropic's own unaudited forecast of $190–200 billion of 2028 revenue** — about 40x its 2025 revenue, inside three years. The forecast is a projection. The **$518 billion** is a contract, and about 80% of it is payable whether the forecast arrives or not. The valuation is not expensive against the plan. It is entirely dependent on it, and the obligations are not.

And there is no cash-flow line to check it against. No operating cash flow, no burn, no runway has been reported anywhere. The $20.28 billion cash figure is as of 31 December 2025 and predates **$95 billion** of equity raised since, so no runway can be inferred from it either.

**Now the scenario, flagged as a scenario.** A confidential draft S-1 went in on 1 June. Reuters saw a draft in late September. Bloomberg reports a roadshow possibly the week of **9 November**. A confidential filer must make its registration statement public **at least 15 days before the roadshow** — so a 9 November roadshow requires a **public S-1 by about 25 October.** I re-checked on **15 October** and there is none: EDGAR returns 35 filers whose names contain "Anthropic" and every one is a fund or series vehicle buying its shares, not the company. So the 14 October investor day told prospective buyers a revenue figure that the registration record still does not contain, ten days before the deadline that a 9 November roadshow would require. That is the date to watch, and it is falsifiable rather than atmospheric.

**If Anthropic does list at a $2 trillion valuation before the end of 2026, I put the probability of a subsequent de-rating that drags the broader AI trade with it above 50%.** That is a judgement, not a disclosure, and it is the least verifiable sentence in this series. Three transmission mechanisms make it more than a feeling:

- **Index and benchmark inclusion.** A listing at that size enters passive portfolios mechanically. Price discovery stops being optional for anyone who owns the index.
- **Mark-to-market on private stakes.** Microsoft, Alphabet and Amazon all carry AI positions whose value is referenced to private rounds. A public print re-prices the reference.
- **Backlog concentration.** [Part One](ninety-six-cents.html) showed Microsoft's RPO growing 84% with OpenAI and 25% without it. A named counterparty re-rating downward re-prices the backlog that justified the capex — the same backlog this post has already shown is one customer in large part.

The counter-case is that the window closes before the test. **Seven sizable US IPOs were pulled or postponed in Q3 2026 against four in Q2 and three in Q1** — a curated count I have not been able to corroborate from primary data. The registration record does show 2026 running materially worse than 2025 for withdrawals by actual registrants, but it puts the deterioration in Q2 rather than Q3; [Part Four](ninety-six-cents-part-four.html) sets out the method and its limits. The cleaner datapoint is a single deal: NVIDIA-backed Firmus shelved a **$5 billion** listing on 9 October, Sydney time. That was an Australian listing, not a US one, and reading it as a signal about AI valuations is Reuters' framing rather than the company's. It went to a private round instead. The scenario needs the listing to happen to matter.

**Which generalises the concentration point.** Microsoft's +84%-versus-+25% gap is not a Microsoft quirk. Amazon in particular is more exposed to renting compute without a first-party reservoir of its own demand, so backlog concentration in the labs hands those labs bargaining power at renewal. A backlog is only as good as the counterparty's ability to keep paying for capacity it contracted before it had the revenue.

### The other lab, which is not the safe one

The natural move here is to say OpenAI is the stronger counterparty. I went looking for that and found something narrower: **OpenAI is diversified on supply and not on revenue, and it is the more committed of the two, not the less.**

Start with the commitments, because they are the whole argument in [Part One](ninety-six-cents.html). Anthropic's are **at least $518 billion**. OpenAI's **company-stated contracts alone** come to about **$688 billion** — **$300 billion** with Oracle, an incremental **$250 billion** of Azure, and **$138 billion** with AWS. Its own spend plan is **about $750 billion through 2030**, and a deck reported in September puts compute and infrastructure at **$856 billion across 2026–2030 against $278 billion of negative free cash flow.** On every basis available, OpenAI's number is the bigger one. On none is it smaller.

Three caveats before anyone leans on that. Anthropic's figure carries a stated cancelability split and OpenAI's does not. OpenAI has shown it will walk away from capacity — Abilene declined, Cobalt Park suspended, Narvik reconfigured. And the ~$1.05 trillion aggregate of announced deals that circulates is almost certainly double-counted, because Broadcom and AMD silicon gets deployed inside cloud capacity OpenAI is also paying for. The comparison is directionally sound rather than like-for-like.

**Now the revenue, and the measurement trap that makes most comparisons worthless.** OpenAI's annualised revenue was about **$50 billion at the end of September**. Anthropic's was **$65 billion at the end of July**. Those are not comparable: **OpenAI reports net of sales through cloud partners and Anthropic reports gross, including AWS and Google Cloud resale.** The $70 billion OpenAI figure that circulated in late September was produced by its own investors grossing it up to match Anthropic's basis; a source familiar with the documents told CNN it had not come from OpenAI, and the correction on 8 October took NVIDIA, Oracle and CoreWeave down with it. A $20 billion move in nine days, with nothing happening to the business.

What is booked is worse than the run-rate implies. 2025 revenue **$13.07 billion** against a **$20.9 billion** operating loss. Q2 2026 revenue **$6.7 billion** against a **$12.3 billion** operating loss — so first-half operating losses already exceed all of 2025. As with Anthropic, **no operating cash flow figure exists anywhere.**

**The diversification is thinner than the narrative.** Ads are real: ChatGPT Ads went to a US pilot in February and OpenAI announced a **$1 billion annualised** run rate on 31 August, inside 200 days. That is a genuine new line — and about **2% of revenue**, against OpenAI's own $2.5 billion target for the year.

Commerce is worse than thin. Instant Checkout launched in September 2025, charged a 4% fee from January, and was **retired on 24 March 2026**. OpenAI's own merchant page now says purchases "are completed on merchant-owned websites or apps" and, decisively, **"There are no fees on purchases that start in ChatGPT."** Commerce contributes approximately nothing. Amazon has blocked dozens of shopping agents including ChatGPT's, and Walmart found in-chat purchases converted at about a third the rate of click-throughs to its own site.

Enterprise is the strongest leg. The CFO told investors in August that **"the majority of our revenue is now enterprise,"** two quarters ahead of plan, with enterprise run-rate growing **107%** against 77% for the total. Set against that, Menlo Ventures puts OpenAI's share of enterprise LLM usage at **27% at the end of 2025, down from 50% in 2023**, with Anthropic at 40%. Every seat count in circulation — three million, seven million, nine million, "92% of the Fortune 500" — traces to aggregators and none of it is sourced. I am not using any of it.

**Where OpenAI genuinely is stronger is supply, and it is not close.** Six capacity and silicon partners: Broadcom 10GW, AMD 6GW, NVIDIA 5GW, Oracle 4.5GW, AWS 2GW, Cerebras 0.75GW. **Jalapeño**, its first custom chip, was unveiled with Broadcom in June — an *inference* processor, 216 GiB of HBM4, at engineering-sample stage with first deployment targeted for end-2026, not volume. Anthropic has nothing equivalent. If the thesis of this series is that position in the chain decides who survives, OpenAI has bought more positions.

Two structures in that supply book are routinely reported backwards, and both are in counterparty filings rather than OpenAI's.

**The AMD "stake" is not a stake.** OpenAI holds a warrant for up to 160 million AMD shares at one cent, vesting in tranches against *both* purchase milestones *and* AMD share-price thresholds, each tranche subject to further conditions. AMD's last filed quarter is explicit: **"As of June 27, 2026, no warrant shares had vested or become exercisable."** The "10% of AMD" headline is the fully-vested ceiling. And AMD issued **Meta an identical 160-million-share warrant** in February, so it is not even exclusive.

**And on Cerebras, OpenAI is the lender.** Cerebras's own 10-Q discloses a December 2025 agreement for 750MW that OpenAI is contractually committed to buy — and **a roughly $1 billion secured promissory note funded by OpenAI**, with $918.2 million outstanding. The money flows from the customer to the supplier. The press figures for that deal — $10 billion, then over $20 billion, then "up to $30 billion" — are mutually inconsistent and none is the contract value.

**The consumer hardware is not a phone.** OpenAI bought Jony Ive's io for about $6.5 billion in 2025. A sworn declaration in the iyO litigation states the first device **will not ship before the end of February 2027**, that no packaging or marketing exists, and an earlier filing rules out a wearable or in-ear product. Reporting converges on a roughly $200–300 portable screenless speaker with a camera. Nothing has been unveiled and it has no public name. Meanwhile **Apple sued OpenAI, io and OpenAI's own chief hardware officer on 10 July** for trade-secret misappropriation, alleging more than 400 former Apple employees now work there.

So: OpenAI is the better-supplied counterparty and the more heavily committed one, with one real new revenue line at 2% of the total and a commerce business that charges nothing. It is not the safe one. It is the one with more ways to get the compute and the same problem paying for it.

## Who actually gets paid

The moat question is the one worth asking, and there is now live data on it.

Vercel is a hosting platform for web applications, and the company behind Next.js — the React framework a large share of the web’s front ends are built on. Its AI Gateway is a single API those applications call instead of wiring up each model provider separately, and it routes tens of trillions of tokens a month between production apps and the labs. Vercel publishes the usage data openly, updated daily, under a Creative Commons licence with a public API. So this is not commentary. It is production traffic, and anyone can download it.

Three things in it.

**First, open-weight models have taken the volume.** On Vercel's published measure, open-weight models ran **56% of all gateway tokens in August 2026** — the first month they took a majority — up from **7% in December 2025**. On the narrower four-lab measure I computed from their raw export — DeepSeek, MiniMax, Moonshot and Z.ai — the same series runs **11.7% in April to 42.5% in August to 64.6% in September**.

**Second, they have not taken the money.** Those same open-weight models accounted for about **14 cents of every dollar** spent through the gateway in August. Volume share and revenue share are completely different things, and most coverage of the open-source shift conflates them.

**Third — and this is new — Anthropic's grip on the dollars broke in September.** Vercel's own report notes Anthropic's share of gateway spend had never fallen below 61% in any month since December 2025. I pulled their raw export on 10 October and computed the monthly series:

| Month | Anthropic share of gateway spend |
|---|---:|
| Mar 2026 | 83.9% |
| Jun 2026 | 62.0% |
| Jul 2026 | 65.6% |
| Aug 2026 | 64.1% |
| **Sep 2026** | **45.8%** |
| Oct 2026 (first 9 days) | 40.6% |

The daily series shows a steady slide through September — 56.5% on the 1st, around 40% by month end, a low of 32.8% on the 22nd. It is not one bad day.

**But the decomposition matters, and it cuts against the simple story.** OpenAI's share of gateway spend rose from 12.7% to 22.1% over the same month. GPT-6 Astra launched on 3 September and took a third of OpenAI's gateway spend within 48 hours. So roughly half of what Anthropic lost in September went to a **cheaper frontier competitor**, not to open source.

Put the three together and Anthropic is being displaced on three fronts at once: a cheaper rival at the frontier, open-weight models underneath, and its largest customers building in-house. That is a different problem from losing a price war.

**The same pattern shows up in the physical layer.** Memory is the binding constraint, Micron is one of only three companies that can supply it at the leading edge, and its share of the highest-value product is falling. Counterpoint puts Micron's HBM revenue share at **18% in Q2 2026, down from 21%**, while Samsung went from **21% to 33%** and SK hynix slipped from 58% to 50%. Management's stated goal is an HBM share close to its DRAM share, which is about 24% — so the gap is moving the wrong way. Those are third-party estimates on a revenue basis, not Micron's own disclosure, and Micron does not publish the number. The direction is the point: being inside the bottleneck is not the same as capturing it. Same lesson as the gateway data, one layer down in physical plant.

**The reason it can happen this fast is structural, and Vercel says it plainly.** Teams running more than 10 million requests use an average of **35 different models**. Vercel's own conclusion: *"Every one of those models is swappable… switching between labs is closer to a config change than to a vendor migration, and the standard story about lab lock-in inverts the higher you go on the request-volume curve."*

If you want proof that a frontier lab can be displaced quickly, look at Google. Its share of gateway token volume fell from **39% in April to 5% in September.** Gemini 3 Flash lost 95% of its share, and more than three-quarters of the volume it lost went to other labs.

There is one more tell inside Anthropic's own numbers. **Fable 5, its most capable model, fell from 13.2% of gateway spend to 4.9% in a single month**, while Opus 5 — roughly half the price — rose to 22.5%. Nine in ten teams running Fable cut their usage. Anthropic kept the dollar, but it kept it by letting customers trade down inside its own range. Average price per token across the whole gateway fell **23.2% in August**, the third consecutive monthly drop.

So demand is not the problem. Demand is obviously there. What is moving is *where* it is served and *what it costs* — and both move against the company carrying $518 billion of non-cancelable commitments priced on the old economics.

*Two caveats, and they are real. These are share figures, not absolute — the export Vercel publishes carries no volumes at all, only shares. Vercel's own commentary reports gateway volume rising month on month, so Anthropic's absolute spend may still be rising, and its reported run-rate did keep climbing through September. And AI Gateway is one channel, weighted toward developers and app builders. It is a sharp read on where production inference is going, not a measurement of total enterprise AI spend. October is nine complete days of data, the dailies in it range from 31.4% to 45.3%, and Vercel restates prior days as data completes — the 5 October figure moved from 45.9% to 45.2% between two pulls a day apart, then held at 45.2% in a third.*

## What Part Two settles

**The moat is thinner than the backlog implies.** This is the part I have changed my mind on while writing. Anthropic's share of production spend on Vercel's gateway held between 62% and 84% in every one of the eleven months to August, then fell to 45.8% in September. Open-weight models took the majority of tokens. Microsoft and Meta started moving their own engineers off Claude. A frontier lab's position can be re-cut in a quarter, because at scale a model is a config entry, not an installed system. Demand being real is not the same as any particular supplier capturing it — and the capex in this post was committed on the assumption that it would.

So the counterparty is the fragile part, not the plant. The cash in [Part One](ninety-six-cents.html) is spent and the assets exist. What is uncertain is whether the entity whose growth justified them can carry the bill, and what its equity is worth when the market finally prices it. Which leaves the question this series has to answer: if the counterparty breaks, does the infrastructure go with it? That is [Part Three](ninety-six-cents-part-three.html).
